OUR STORY

Stop guessing.
Start testing.

You've seen the tests all over TikTok — "tell me something only a real friend would know", "say bird", "peel this orange". They get millions of views because they produce something a quiz never can: a real reaction from a real person.

The Test Library turns those moments into something you can actually run, record, and share. We're the script, the scorekeeper, and the share card — the test happens in your real life, not in a form on a screen.

Three ways to play

Single-player tests. Bird Theory, Orange Peel, Rage Bait Questions. You run the test on someone in person, pick what they did, and get a verdict you can share. Each test comes with a script, the backstory, and a result card.

Green or Red. Our two-player mode. One person answers six questions about themselves; the other guesses. The verdict only unlocks when both of you have played — so you have to send the link to someone. It's a test of how well you know each other, wrapped in a reason to talk.

Aura Score. A daily vibe check. Read a real-life scenario, guess whether it gains or drains aura, then see the verdict. There's a daily question, a ten-scene library, and a five-round run if you want a proper score.

Why real life?

Most personality quizzes ask you to describe yourself, then tell you who you are. That's fun for about thirty seconds. The tests that actually spread are the ones with a reaction — a partner rolling their eyes, a friend laughing, someone missing the hint and proving the point.

So we flipped the structure. The website doesn't generate the moment; it helps you create one and then judge what happened. You do the test face to face, come back to record the response, and the site turns it into a card worth posting. The best part of any test is what someone does when they think you're joking — we just give that part a scoreboard.

How we pick tests

We don't invent trends — we find things people are already doing and give them a clean way to play. A test makes it onto the site only if it passes four filters:

  • It needs a reaction, not just an answer. If the whole thing could be replaced by a multiple-choice quiz on a screen, it isn't for us. The good ones need a real person doing something in front of you.
  • It takes under three minutes. These are things you can try at dinner, on a walk, or while waiting for coffee. Anything longer stops being a test and starts being an assignment.
  • There's a claim worth checking. Every trend carries an implicit theory — that a follow-up question means care, that a peeled orange means service, that posture means power. We look at what the claim actually rests on and say so in the result.
  • It survives the "is this mean?" check. A test that only works by making someone feel exposed isn't a good test. If the fun depends on one person losing, we pass.

That last filter is why some of the biggest trends never made it here. A shareable moment is not the same thing as a good one.

How we write verdicts

Every result on this site has three layers, and they do different jobs.

The headline is the immediate read — a few words you could screenshot. The description translates that into what actually happened between you two. The notes below it explain where the idea comes from, and where it doesn't hold.

That last layer is the one we spend the most time on. A relationship test that says "green flag" and stops there is just a fortune cookie. So we write what the research actually says, name the studies when there are any, and say plainly when there aren't. If a trend has no evidence behind it — the Green Line Theory, for example — the verdict says so rather than dressing it up.

We also write the caveats into the result itself. You'll see "one moment isn't a pattern" a lot. That's deliberate: it's the most honest thing we can tell you, and it's the part most viral tests leave out.

The science, honestly

We borrow from real research, and we think it's worth knowing where it comes from:

  • Bird Theory and Orange Peel trace back to the Gottman Institute's work on "bids for connection" — the small invitations partners make to each other, and whether they turn toward or away. In long-term studies, couples who stayed together responded to these bids far more often than couples who split.
  • Green or Red draws on the idea of "love maps" — how well partners know the details of each other's inner world.
  • Aura Score is pure internet folklore. It's a language people already use to joke about social behavior, and we're playing with it, not claiming it's a science.

Here's the caveat that matters: every one of these tests is a single moment. Real psychologists will tell you that one moment is not a pattern. We built the caveats right into the verdicts, because a test that tells you your relationship is doomed over an orange peel isn't a test — it's a bad joke.

For fun, not a diagnosis

That line appears in the footer of every page for a reason. These tests are conversation starters. A green flag is not a guarantee, and a red flag is not a verdict. If a test surfaces something that actually bothers you, the useful move is to talk about it — not to take a screenshot.

Use them to laugh, to start a conversation, to settle a silly argument about who knows who better. Don't use them to decide whether to stay with someone.

Who's behind this

The Test Library is a small, independent project — one person, a lot of research tabs, and no advertising agenda beyond keeping the site online. It started with a simple frustration: the tests worth trying were scattered across short-form video clips, captioned in ways that were hard to follow and impossible to redo. There was no place that collected them, cleaned them up, and told you what they actually meant.

So that's what this is. A library, in the literal sense: a catalog of tests you can pull off the shelf, run in real life, and put back with a verdict attached. The site is deliberately lightweight — no account, no email capture, no tracking you need a degree to understand. Your results stay in your browser, and the sharing happens only when you choose it.

We read the research so you don't have to, and we write the caveats so you can decide for yourself. If a verdict ever seems too confident, that's a bug — tell us and we'll fix it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an account? No. Nothing here requires signing up. Your answers are stored locally in your browser so a refresh doesn't wipe them, and that's the extent of it.

Are these tests scientifically validated? No — and we'd be suspicious of anything that claimed to be. They're conversation starters built around real ideas in relationship research. A single moment can't diagnose a relationship, and every result on this site says so.

Do you see my answers? No. There's no server storing them. When you share a result, only the result and the test ID travel in the link — never the other person's private answers.

Can I suggest a test? Please do. The best entries here came from someone saying "you should test this". Email us — if it passes the filters above, it'll get added.

Is this only for couples? Most tests are written for two people who know each other well, and romance is the common setting. But nothing about them requires a partner — friends and siblings run them too.

Why does the site have ads? To cover hosting and the domain. They don't influence which tests appear or what the verdicts say, and that separation isn't negotiable.
